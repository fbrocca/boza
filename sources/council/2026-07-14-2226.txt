6.A. Transportation Committee — Discussion and Potential Vote on
Charleston County Highway 41 Corridor Improvement Project
Motion and Initial Procedural Discussion
Council Member Jake Rambo made a motion to deny municipal consent
specifically for the Laurel Hill Parkway component of the project. Mayor
Haynie raised a point of order and asked Town Legal Counsel David
Pagliarini to clarify the scope of municipal consent authority under State
law, specifically Sections 57-05-820 and 57-05-830. Mr. Pagliarini
confirmed that municipalities may only withhold consent within their own
political boundaries of incorporation, and that Laurel Hill Park is located
in unincorporated Charleston County, not within the Town of Mount
Pleasant.
Council Member Rambo clarified that his motion was not directed at the
entirety of the project but specifically at the connection of the Laurel Hill
Parkway to town-owned right of way — namely Dunes West Boulevard
— which falls within the town's jurisdiction. Mr. Pagliarini confirmed that
the motion, as stated, applied appropriately to the withholding of permits
or approvals for connections to town rights of way.

● The 1990 town plan for Dunes West development already called
  for a four-lane Highway 41, a five-lane Park West Boulevard, and a
  four-lane Dunes West Boulevard, with a note that Park West
  Boulevard might ultimately need six lanes.
● Approximately 30 percent of the town's population lives in the
  affected corridor.
● In the past ten years, Dunes West, Park West, and Carolina Park
  added nearly 3,500 homes.
● Growth projections include approximately 12,000 new homes
  coming to the area, with roughly 25 percent of those trips projected
  to use Highway 41. The traffic model was last substantially
  updated in 2024.
● The process involved nine years of work, more than 200 meetings,
  more than 7,300 public comments, and evaluation of over 20
  alternatives.
● In 2020, the county recommended Alternative 1 — full four-lane
  widening along the entirety of Highway 41 — as the preferred
  alternative. 794 comments were received opposing it, specifically
  because of the impact to the Phillips community. Based on that
  feedback, the county directed the engineering team to stay within
  the existing 75-foot right of way through the Phillips community,
  limiting improvements in that section to a two-way center turn lane.

● Alternative 7, which would have widened Dunes West Boulevard
  and Bessemer Road to five lanes, was also rejected following
  community outcry and a formal objection from the town.
● The road to compromise emerged as the solution that avoided
  widening Dunes West, Park West, and Highway 41 through
  Phillips — it was called a compromise precisely because each of
  those communities gave something.
● The 4-3-4 design alone (without Laurel Hill Parkway) was modeled
  to fail within five to seven years of opening, reaching level of
  service E or F, with traffic on Bessemer Road backing up nearly a
  mile to Dunes West Boulevard.
● The Laurel Hill Parkway adds intersection capacity and is
  projected to attract approximately 12,000 vehicles per day by 2045
  — comparable to Rifle Range Road. It is expected to draw
  commuter traffic away from Bessemer Road and relieve
  congestion at the Highway 41/Park West intersection.
● The project is projected to operate at an acceptable level of
  service D in 2045 — twenty years after projected completion.
● The parkway would affect 10 acres of the 741-acre park, impact 2
  acres of wetlands, and would not affect any existing trails. Four
  hundred trees would be planted along the corridor.
● The total project cost is $245 million, the largest infrastructure
  investment in the town's history. $15 million has already been
  spent, and $63 million has been committed from the State
  Transportation Infrastructure Bank (STIB). The county noted it is
  not currently in compliance with its STIB agreement due to a
  missed construction start deadline and has requested an extension
  without yet receiving a response.
● Mr. Reidenbach stated that if the project were redesigned or
  significantly delayed, there was a real risk that competing
  jurisdictions would pursue the STIB funds. He noted that 171
  parcels of right of way were being acquired with zero total takes
  (no homes being demolished).
● Alternative routing via Bessemer Road was studied in 2021 and
  2022 but was found to require acquisition from approximately 60
  residential properties, significant utility relocation, and cost more
  than the Laurel Hill Parkway option, while delivering inferior traffic
  performance due to the number of driveways and curb cuts along
  Bessemer.

Council Questions and Discussion
Council Member Rambo questioned the engineering team about the cost
and capability of Alternative 1 as originally presented in 2020, noting it
had been projected at approximately $125 million and would have
addressed the problem more directly. He noted that the current plan, at
$245 million, was effectively a more expensive partial solution. He also
raised that no formal presentation had been made to the full council
since 2020, and that the road to compromise had never been formally
presented to this body.
Mr. Reidenbach acknowledged that inflation — estimated at
approximately 75 percent for construction costs since COVID —
accounts for much of the cost escalation, and that Alternative 1 at
today's prices would likely have approached $200 million. He also
acknowledged that the constraints on the design team were driven by
county council direction following community feedback.
Council Member Kathryn Whitaker asked Mr. Reidenbach to confirm the
facts regarding the necessity of the parkway, the acreage affected, and
trail impacts. He confirmed: 10 acres of parkland, 2 acres of wetlands,
and zero trail impacts.
Council Member Daniel Brownstein asked a series of detailed questions,
including: whether Alternative 1 remained the best traffic solution
(confirmed); the length of construction (4.5 years); the projected level of
service at completion (better than D, with D reached in 2045); what the
next transportation solution would likely look like after this project
reaches capacity; whether widening through Phillips would be feasible in
the future given National Register protections (possible but difficult and
requiring a lengthy environmental process); how much the engineering
team would have to encroach into the Phillips community to fix the Joe
Rouse/Bessemer and Highway 41 intersection (approximately 1,500
feet); whether expanding the road to four lanes within the existing 75-
foot right of way through Phillips was geometrically feasible (not safely
without exceeding the right of way once shoulders, sidewalks, center
turn lanes, and drainage are accounted for); and where wetland
mitigation credits would be purchased (a saltwater mitigation bank at
Point Farm in Wadmalaw Island, with freshwater credits yet to be
determined).

Director of Planning, Land Use, and Neighborhoods Michele Reed
briefly explained the amendment: the current area median income cap
for attainable units in Gregory Ferry Towns is 80 percent of AMI,
equivalent to $94,000. The request was to increase it to 150 percent of
AMI, consistent with comparable developments such as Carolina Park
Towns.

indicated he did not support the full 150 percent threshold but wished to
pass first reading to allow the Planning Committee to further examine
the appropriate level. Council Member Iacofano similarly suggested 120
percent AMI — which would bring the qualifying income to approximately
$144,000 — as a more appropriate figure and recommended that the
formula better account for fluctuating interest rates. Council Member
Hyatt noted that 120 percent is still generally considered workforce
housing, while 150 percent is not.
