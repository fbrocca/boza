2.       Public Comment
           David Quick, 1502 Village Square, stated he is concerned
     about the tree situation in the Town, specifically regarding the
     Dominion Energy trimming of the trees. He said there are problems
     with tree protection in the town, with too many trees being cut down,
     and our canopy is being destroyed. He said he hoped the tree
     ordinances and protection changes are being taken seriously. He
     said we need to think about adding staff to police the ordinances and
     the trees.
           Jamie McKee 1247 Palmetto Peninsula Drive stated he
     agreed with Mr. Quick, and thinks the policy is a great idea. He said
     his neighbor just received approval to cut down a healthy twenty-inch
     live oak tree, and he thinks if exceptions are going to be allowed, then
     there is not really a policy any longer. He suggested that Council
     consider how to measure enforcement, the accountability level, how
     many exceptions have been approved and why. He said it is very
     difficult to replace twenty-five-year-old trees.
          Mike Tinkey, 208 Venning street stated he wants to speak
     about the importance of having a holistic approach to trees. He said
     we have been talking about the ordinance, but without enforcement,
     and staffing, there is no point. He said we have a strategic plan for
     our built environment, but we do not have one for our trees. He said
     in basic forestry the trees are counted, and he would like to ask
     Council to fully fund the urban forestry program that specifically count
     all the trees to know what sizes they are and the type, so that we can
     deal with it, along with the permits issued for removal. Mr. Tinkey
     said he had looked at the ordinance and several things could be
     updated, such as increasing the fees for mitigation, and coordination
     between our staff and various departments as there are
     inconsistencies. He added the training and qualifications of staff are
     very important. Mr. Tinkey said lastly the enforcement is ineffectual
     as far as the penalties. He said there needs to be enforcement for
     companies cutting trees illegally.

Ms. Reed gave the following presentation regarding the tree
changes and stated Chris Lubert and Eddie Bernard were in
attendance should there be any questions.
Mr. Chapman said another issue that arises is the Board of
Zoning Appeals (BOZA), and he does not know how to get
around that as it is their job to give authorization for appeals to
our Zoning ordinance. Ms. Reed said there are those who
apply to the Board of Zoning Appeals, to appeal their mitigation
amount, and there are also instances where people are
ticketed, and through the court process it is reduced or
dismissed. Ms. Reed said they are trying to collect the fees on
the front end.
Ms. Reed continued with the presentation.
Mayor said one of the issues he has is to classify all pines as
the same is not the most educated way to do this. He said
there are many different species, and a long-leaf pine is much
more storm resistant than a loblolly pine. He said the other
question is where did the 24-inch diameter come from, and is
that a number that is relevant to what we are dealing with today
in terms of resiliency.
Mr. Bernard said he did not know the answer, and before the
2000 changes, it was always 8, 16 and 24 inches that were
always our thresholds. He said the City of Charleston and
other communities use that as well. He said he is not aware of
any scientific reason as to why it is 24 inches. Mayor Haynie
said at the state level they have just banned in the future
Bradford Pears, and he said thirty years ago, they were being
planted everywhere, and presently it has been determined
these are not good trees to have around. Mr. Bernard said in
the ordinances, 24 inches have always been considered
historic trees, and it made sense to protect the larger ones.
Mr. Bernard said the live crown ratio is the proportion of canopy
to trunk. He said when you look at an open grown pine versus
forested pines, the forested pines are going to use all their
energy in growing taller, and being skinnier, where as an open
grown pine has ample sun, so it will leave a lot of its lower limbs
and have a better taper.
Ms. Whitley asked if the theory is that because the tree made it
to 24 inches, it will continue to grow. Mr. Bernard said it is
made that size because of how it was developed. Ms. Whitley
said her concern is that when it is right next to a home, if it falls
it will crush the house. She said she feels that pines just tend
to snap more, and perhaps if one species is better than
another, then we could protect the ones that are more stable.
Mayor said if a homeowner wanted to remove a pine tree for
reasons of safety, could there be a matrix such as what type of
pine is it, its live crown ratio and taper, and what is the proximity
to the house, and the age and health of the tree.
Ms. Reed said if Council decides to protect pine trees, we will
treat them like every other tree, and there is a matrix and
process in place. She asked Mr. Lubert to review the existing
process.
Mr. Lubert said only good and fair trees are the ones that
receive more scrutiny, and anything that we feel is in poor
condition, classified as a hazard tree would be allowed to be
removed. He said we do not want to get in a situation of
keeping bad trees, but we want a good, healthy canopy. He
said we are looking at not only the health, but the site, as well
as the utility conflicts, looking at the whole picture, not just one
item at a time. He said they take into consideration how close it
is to the home, and can anything be reasonably done to
mitigate the damage, as every tree is going to have a certain
amount of danger.
Mr. Iacofano asked if every tree receives a site visit, and Mr.
Lubert responded in the affirmative, and there could be up to
four trees on one tree removal request. Mr. Lubert stated he
could do up to twenty tree inspections per day and requests are
seven to ten days out.
Ms. Reed said every request gets an inspection and if they are
going to replant, that would be a follow up inspection. She said
there is a very detailed process where trees are allowed to be
removed, and typically they are hazardous. Ms. Reed said
even if a tree is unhealthy and allowed to be removed,
mitigation is still required.
Mr. Iacofano asked what would happen if someone’s tree was
beginning to fall down, and Mr. Lubert responded they would
work to expedite the process. Mr. Iacofano asked how many
days that would delay the process if there were no additional
staff members to cover these changes. Ms. Reed said calls
were put out to some area tree removal companies to see how
many calls they receive about pine trees, and based on the
information received, she stated it would probably increase the
workload by about 20-25 percent.
Mr. Iacofano asked, besides the large land clearing, how many
pine trees are being taken out currently. Ms. Yager-Baumrind
stated about 20 a month per company. Ms. Reed stated there
are about 5-6 companies, but we do not know how many of
those are 20 inches or greater.
Ms. Hyatt asked if the 24 inches came from the arborist, and
Ms. Reed responded in the negative, stating that information
came from public meetings, and Council will need to tell staff
whether or not this draft will move forward. She stated this is
not a staff recommendation.
Ms. Hyatt asked if this information is acceptable to the
arborists. Mr. Lubert responded in the affirmative. Mr. Bernard
concurred.
Mayor Haynie said the budget for this fiscal year just started,
but if we need to add one or two people to help with trees, it will
be much less expensive than what it will cost to fix flooding.
Mr. Chapman said he would like to read some things for which
trees are useful. He said they are used for promoting the public
health, safety and general welfare; lessen air pollution; increase
dust filtration; reduce noise, heat, and glare; prevent soil
erosion; improve surface drainage and minimize flooding;
ensure that noise, glare, and other distractions of movement in
one area do not adversely affect activity within other adjacent
areas; beautify and enhance. He said that is what we are
working on in terms of resilience, but most importantly to
protect all trees.
Mr. Chapman moved to proceed with the tree portion, including
pine trees, as presented, seconded by Ms. Hyatt.
Mr. Brownstein said his concern is that we are stepping in
between a homeowner who feels like a tree is going to fall on
their house. He asked who determines whether the tree is in
good, fair or poor condition. Mr. Lubert said he goes based on
the ordinance, and if it is obvious, he feels comfortable making
the determination, but if it is not, we will defer to an arborist.
Mr. Brownstein said trees are not inexpensive to remove, and
he feels that if someone is removing a tree on their lot, it is
because there is a problem or a perceived problem with it and
he is uncomfortable inserting ourselves into that situation,
especially given the public sentiment about pine trees. He said
this is a tough call, and asked how long it would take for a pine
tree to grow into a 24-inch caliper. Mr. Lubert said there are
many factors but estimated twenty years. Mr. Brownstein
asked how that would compare to an oak tree. Mr. Lubert said
it would be similar.
Mr. Brownstein said he thinks a lot of the reaction to the pine
trees is because of the clear-cutting at Carolina Park, and he
wished there was a way to differentiate between a pine tree on
an existing single-family lot versus a developer clear-cutting.
Ms. Reed said there is, and all we are talking about right now is
single family lots. She said Carolina Park is totally different as
it has a development agreement and it is a planned
development, but if we have a new subdivision, for example a
20-acre subdivision like Carolina Park, that would go to the
Planning Commission, and they approve removal of all trees.
She said they would make the approvals based on whatever is
adopted by Council, so if 24 inches and greater are to be
protected, then they would have to show that, and if removal
was approved, they would need to mitigate those just like any
other 24 inch tree.
Mr. Brownstein asked if there is any way to protect pine trees
on a new development, but not protect them on an existing
single-family lot.
Mr. Rambo asked if we could protect them at 24 inches, but
made an exception where if the tree is within the fall radius of a
residence it would not require a permit for removal.
Ms. Reed said staff has a process in place for protected trees if
they are a hazard to a house already, and in her opinion, it
would be better to let them review and regulate them as they
would the other trees.
Mayor said there was an incident in town where someone
denuded a commercial lot at one time, and a tree service
company, unlicensed in the Town, said they were all pines that
could be cut down, but they were part of a required buffer in
that area.
Ms. Reed said if someone applied to remove a tree and the
justification for requested removal was that it was a hazard, that

Ms. Hyatt said now by protecting them, this would provide
