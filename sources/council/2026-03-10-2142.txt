4. Public Hearings, Awards, & Presentations
A. Public Hearing: A Public Hearing to receive input on a proposal to
amend Chapter 155, Land Development Regulations of the Mount
Pleasant Code of Ordinances, Town of Mount Pleasant is proposing to
amend Chapter 155.054 GREENSPACE PRESERVATION AND
PROTECTION PLAN, AND ENVIRONMENTAL GUIDELINES FOR
LAND DEVELOPMENT to include the preservation of isolated wetlands
and environmentally sensitive areas. Through the adoption of a local
ordinance, all subdivision requests along with site plans for residential
and non-residential developments shall be evaluated for features
susceptible to sustaining aquatic vegetation, hydric soils, and wildlife
found in wetlands. As a result of the 2023 US Supreme Court ruling,
these areas are not classified as Section 404 Jurisdictional Waters of the
US, and therefore, unprotected from development. This ordinance is
proposing to identify and preserve the ecological benefit these areas
uniquely provide to our citizens by restricting fill in perpetuity upon all
building permit and subdivision requests.
Kevin Mitchell presented the amendment to chapter 155.054 to change
the green space preservation protection plan and environmental
guidelines for land development to include the protection of isolated
wetlands. As this was in the land development regulation, Town Council
was required to hold a public hearing.
Mayor Haynie opened the public hearing, clarifying that comments
during the public hearing would not count against speakers' time during
regular public comment.
Public Comments:
Michael Ramsey, representing the Charleston Trident Association of
Realtors with more than 2,300 realtors across the region and more than
1,500 members living in Mount Pleasant, acknowledged the town's
environmental stewardship efforts but expressed concerns about the
cumulative impact of layering regulations. He noted that as much as 9
percent of Mount Pleasant's land area may contain non-jurisdictional
wetlands. His concerns included overregulation of property owners,
impact on commercial investment creating uncertainty and delays, and
housing affordability issues as regulations increase costs that are
passed on to homeowners and renters.
Monika Bonn Miller completely disagreed with the previous speaker,
emphasizing that trees are necessary for oxygen production and
wetlands are essential for water management. She stated that if you
start building in wetlands, water goes somewhere else, potentially
ending up in foundations, and argued there was nothing to discuss as
preserving wetlands was obvious.
Michelle Stress disagreed with the realtor representative's vested
interests and called out draft item D2, which allows alteration when
serving an overriding public interest as deemed by the director of
engineering and developmental services. She was concerned that if the
town could not follow its own rules and asked homeowners to follow
them, the town should also follow those same rules.
Bob Adams strongly endorsed the regulations, praising staff and
committees for their work. He disagreed that this imposed unfair
regulation, stating there was a gaping hole created by the Supreme
Court decision that the town was invited to address, and hoped council
would pass it that night.
Mike Van Horn supported wetland protection but questioned how the
ordinance would affect the Rifle Range situation and the park
supposedly being built in that wetland area, given the fifty million dollar
bond taxpayers had already approved for that recreation area.
Andrew Wunderley from Charleston Waterkeeper spoke in favor of the
ordinance, calling wetlands the most valuable public infrastructure for
filtering pollution, storing storm water, and providing habitat. He
emphasized that recent federal court decisions had removed protections
for various wetland types, and the ordinance was a thoughtful local
response designed by community members.
Suzie Carlson, an attorney with the Southern Environmental Law
Center and Mount Pleasant resident, supported the proposed ordinance.
She commended the town's hard work and noted Mount Pleasant had
an opportunity to be a state leader on this issue, calling it a practical step
to safeguard both environment and community future, especially
following the decision in SACKETT vs. EPA.
Tori Sanders from the Coastal Conservation League expressed support
for the wetlands ordinance, applauding the town's leadership. She asked
council to reconsider exceptions currently allowed for rural sheds and
garages, noting that while these seemed minor individually, the
cumulative impact of hundreds of sheds, pools, and garages in wetlands
was significant and would increase impervious surfaces, worsen
flooding, and reduce green space that naturally absorbs and stores
water.
Denise Fugo, Housing for All Mount Pleasant executive director,
mentioned that they work with three engineers in affordable living to
ensure proper shading and prevent flooding. She requested that the
town stop using palm trees in South Carolina due to water issues and
unstable earth throughout the state, noting that palm trees do not help
the earth or air much, referencing the College of Charleston's need for
sandbags in front of buildings due to only having palm trees.
There being no further speakers, Mayor Haynie closed the public
hearing.

Kevin Mitchell presented the ordinance to amend chapter 155.054,
green space preservation and protection plan and environmental
guidelines for land development. This ordinance established
development standards for environmental protection of isolated non-
jurisdictional wetlands.
Mr. Mitchell highlighted key components: this pertained only to non-
jurisdictional wetlands; field investigations conducted by qualified
wetlands professionals with minimum five years verified experience;
applied to all subdivision requests and single-family activity requiring
building permits; buffers of 10 feet average, 20 feet with no less than five
feet, with Planning Commission authority to increase buffer size when
necessary for larger tracts and isolated wetlands.
Exceptions included boundary surveys, property line adjustments, and
lot aggregation; overriding public interest when served; and for five acres
and less, any historical traces of wetlands per GIS mapping with owner
affidavit when proposals did not increase property density.
As chair of the Flood Resilience Committee, Mayor Haynie moved for
approval at first reading, noting they had worked on this for months
through planning commission, planning committee, flood resilience
committee, and public hearings. The first draft was never perfect, and
there were things he would like to change at second reading. They were
unanimous in bringing this to first reading and would put it on next
month's committee agenda for council members with concerns about
wording or provisions. Council Member Whitaker seconded the motion.
Council Member Brownstein thanked both committees for their hard
work, noting it was overall good work product protecting vitally important
wetlands. However, he had consternation about applying this to existing
single-family lots, which made sense for subdividing or large commercial
projects where you could move things around, but existing single-family
homeowners wanting to build garages, decks, or sheds might not have
that ability. He requested considering a special council meeting or
workshop so everyone could provide input and get better sense of the
final product prior to second reading.
Council Member Rambo admitted he probably needed a remedial
course, noting the ordinance was very technical and he was not 100
percent sure what defined a wetland. He was concerned people might
be surprised that random areas of their yards were marked as wetlands
and struggled with comprehension, suggesting others might be thinking
the same thing.
Mayor Haynie noted those were reasonable comments and they could
arrange for staff to provide demonstrations they had done showing
wetland impacts and overlaps, using reliable data from three or four
different regulatory bodies.
    Mayor Haynie called for the vote and all present voted in favor.
