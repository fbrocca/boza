Chaplain Rob Dewey addressed the council to commend the Mount
Pleasant Police Department. He described a recent experience in which
he contacted the assistant police chief regarding concerns about unsafe
golf cart and e-bike operation by minors in his neighborhood. He noted
that within two days, a sergeant followed up personally and provided
assurance that the matter was being addressed. He praised the
responsiveness and professionalism of the department.
George Freeman, president of the CAGE organization, raised three
matters. First, he called attention to a 2006 Memorandum of Agreement
signed between the Town of Mount Pleasant and Charleston County,
which included a provision that there would be no changes to his
neighborhood or new roads constructed within it. He noted that the
agreement also included a commitment by the town and county to assist
the community in building a community center, which had not yet
materialized. Second, he addressed ongoing transportation planning,
referencing a proposal to connect Hungry Neck Park West, Sweetgrass
Parkway, and Billy Swails Boulevard to create a continuous corridor. The
CAGE organization recommended extending Billy Swails Boulevard in
the direction of Morgan Creek to create a parallel road that would relieve
peak traffic on Highway 17 between Long Point Road and Porcher's
Bluff.
Adam Neumann addressed the council regarding a request for
consideration related to an accessory dwelling unit (ADU) setback
ordinance and a missed appeal deadline that he attributed to the
negligence of his hired architect, Jeremy Kruska of Kruska Designs. He
stated that after hiring the architect to submit plans by a February
deadline, the architect failed to submit on time, failed to represent him
adequately at a subsequent March special meeting, and ultimately never
filed an appeal on his behalf. He stated that none of his four immediate
neighbors objected to the proposed ADU and garage, and he asked
council for a pathway toward remedies given that his harm resulted from
professional negligence rather than his own inaction.
Mark Fields, speaking as a representative of DRB Group, addressed
the council regarding the Highway 17 North Village PPD proposal. He
noted that DRB had entered into a contract to purchase the subject
property in the latter half of 2024 and had been diligently pursuing its
development. After the planning commission denied the original
proposal, DRB filed supplemental materials on May 18th. He directed
council's attention to a comparative matrix included in those materials
showing what would be permissible under the existing AD-1 zoning
versus what DRB was proposing under the PD rezoning. He did not

Chris Lathbury, identified himself as the owner of the approximately 11-

Norb Chrzanowski, spoke in support of the Highway 17 North Village
project as modified in the May 18th supplemental submission. He cited
three reasons for his support: the project's visual integration with the
surrounding area and quality architecture; the inclusion of eight
affordable housing units built to the same standard as market-rate units,
coordinated with Denise Marie Fugo of Housing for All; and the planned
signalized intersection with a deceleration lane on Highway 17 opposite
the Aldi grocery store, to be provided at no cost to the town.
Chip Wyatt, project manager and designer with Barrier Island
Engineering, provided technical details in support of the project. He
confirmed that a Traffic Impact Analysis had been performed, submitted,
and approved by SCDOT and the Town of Mount Pleasant, and that it
accounted for all components of the development. He also described the
utility planning, noting that gravity sewer stub-outs had been designed to
allow neighboring residents to connect to public sewer without the need
for grinder pumps. He noted that the commercial buildings along
Highway 17 would be approximately 27 feet in height, and that the
townhome height had been reduced from a previously proposed 50 feet
to 40 feet.
Bennett Rogers, speaking as a member of DRB Group, highlighted that
the project proposed 29 percent open space versus the 10 percent
required under prescriptive zoning, and 85 residential units versus a
potential 124 under current AB-2 zoning. He noted the project included a
75-foot buffer from Highway 17 on the commercial side and a pass-
through to Joe Bryant Court right-of-way.
Jay Paul Smith, addressed the affordable housing component, noting
the eight units were developed in collaboration with Housing for All

Director Reed provided background, noting the matter had been before
both the planning commission and planning committee on multiple
occasions. The planning commission had considered various options
and ultimately recommended that a rezoning to match the
comprehensive plan be addressed during the next comprehensive plan
update cycle. The planning committee, however, directed that the item
move forward to Town Council to consider rezoning church and school
properties currently zoned R-1 or R-2 that were designated as Public
Institutional (PI) in the comprehensive plan future land use map.
Director Reed noted that most affected school properties were owned by
Charleston County School District (CCSD), with two owned by Trident,
and that a list of all affected church and school properties had been
included in council's report. She also said that if council chose to
proceed with the rezoning, certain code amendments might be
necessary — for example, the PI District does not permit residential
uses, which could affect parsonages associated with churches.
Council Member Brownstein raised several questions and concerns. He
asked whether the town had rezoned its own properties to PI, and
Director Reed confirmed it had not, noting 17 town-owned properties
were recommended for PI but had not been rezoned. He further asked
whether churches and schools were legally permitted under their
existing residential zoning, and Director Reed confirmed they were —
churches as a special exception through the Board of Zoning Appeals,
and schools as a conditional use subject to design standards.
Brownstein expressed concern about the optics and practical
consequences of the town initiating a rezoning of properties belonging to
churches and schools that had not requested the change. He was
particularly concerned about the certified notification letters and yellow
zoning signs that would appear in front of these properties, which he
believed would cause confusion and alarm among congregations and
school communities. He suggested the potential change from residential
to PI zoning could introduce less compatible uses, citing examples from
the PI district such as lay-down yards, medical campuses, and sports
complexes.
Council Member Whitaker, who had initially put the item forward,
provided context for the proposal. She stated that while it was
uncommon for church or school properties to convert to non-institutional
uses, it had happened twice during her time on council — once involving
a church and once involving a school. She expressed concern that
without the PI designation, these larger parcels would default to
residential zoning if they were ever vacated, and that preserving their PI
                                                          Town Council Meeting
                                                                 June 9, 2026
                                                                Page 21 of 22

designation would better serve long-term community interests consistent
with the comprehensive plan.
Council Member Tinkey clarified that the discussion specifically
concerned PI-1, not PI-2, which would not include the more intensive
uses that had been cited as concerns. He further noted that several of
the affected churches had operated for over 100 years and that the
intent was to ensure that if a church or school ever chose to sell or
subdivide its property, the town council would have the opportunity for
meaningful review rather than the matter proceeding solely through staff
approval. He referenced the prior rezoning of James B. Edwards
Elementary School to PI as an illustrative precedent, where the school
district had to rezone in order to gain flexibility for improvements.
Mayor Haynie reflected on the comprehensive plan process, noting that
the future land use map had been developed over two-plus years of
extensive public engagement, including a committee of approximately 40
residents, and was adopted unanimously in 2020. He expressed support
for the concept but raised practical concerns about initiating a public
hearing process across 163 properties simultaneously, given the volume
of certified letters, posted notices, and public response it would
generate. He suggested it might be preferable to have a preliminary
discussion through committee or the planning commission without
triggering the formal notice requirements.
Director Reed suggested that planning staff could begin outreach
informally to affected property owners to let them know the matter would
be appearing on an agenda before any formal action was taken. Council
Member Rambo argued that without the formal notice process, most
affected parties were unlikely to engage. He noted the item had
appeared on multiple planning committee and commission agendas
without generating public attendance and concluded that the certified
letter and yellow signs were likely necessary to produce meaningful
feedback.
Assistant Town Administrator Christiane Farrell recalled a prior instance
in which the town had initiated rezonings of economic development
properties following the adoption of a comprehensive plan, noting that
the process involved public hearings, certified letters, and mixed results
— some properties were rezoned and some were not.
Legal Counsel David Pagliarini clarified that the planning commission
public hearing was the first required legal step, after which the matter
would proceed to the planning committee and then to council for two full
